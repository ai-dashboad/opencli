# Document compare

A diff shows every character that moved. A person comparing two drafts wants
to know which of those changes mean something. Those are two different
questions, and only the second is worth their time.

## Establish which is which

Say plainly, in the first line, which version you treated as the earlier one
and which as the later, and how you decided. Reversing them inverts every
finding, and a reader who does not know the direction cannot catch the error.

If it cannot be decided from filenames, dates, or content, **ask**.

## Read both in full

Both documents, end to end, before comparing anything. A section that moved is
not a section that changed, and only reading the whole thing tells them apart.

## Sort every change into one of three

**Changes substance.** The change alters what somebody must do, may do, must
pay, or may lose. Any change to:

- an amount, rate, percentage, or currency
- a date, period, notice window, or deadline
- who a party is, or what they are responsible for
- what is included or excluded from scope
- a condition — anything after "unless", "provided that", "except"
- a right to end, renew, transfer, or refuse
- liability, indemnity, warranty, or a cap on any of them

**Might change substance.** You can read it two ways and the readings differ.
Say both readings, in one line each, and let the reader decide. This section
is not a place to hedge — put something here only when you can state two
genuine readings.

**Wording only.** Same meaning, different words. Do not list these one by one.
Give a count and one example.

## For every change in the first two sections

- Where it is. Clause or section number, and the heading.
- The old text and the new text, quoted. Short quotes, but exact.
- What it means, in one sentence, in plain words.

```
§7.2 Termination
  was:  "either party may terminate on 90 days' notice"
  now:  "either party may terminate on 30 days' notice"
  Notice needed to end the agreement drops from three months to one.
```

## What is missing counts

A clause present in the earlier version and absent from the later one is a
change in substance, and it is the one most often missed — nothing in the new
document points at it. Check for removals deliberately, by walking the earlier
document's sections and finding each one in the later.

## Never

- Never say whether a change is good, acceptable, or standard. That is the
  reader's judgement, and it depends on which side they are on.
- Never recommend signing, refusing, or negotiating anything.
- Never quote from memory. Every quotation comes from the file in front of
  you, and if the file is unreadable, say so rather than working around it.
- Never treat this as legal advice, and say so once at the end.
